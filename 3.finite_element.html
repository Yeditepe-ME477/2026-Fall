(\(\S1.8\)) Space of Piecewise Linear Functions

\(\leftarrow\) Previous Lecture

  • The bar example of the preceeding section employed so-called piecewise linear functions \(N_A(x)\). Spaces \(\mathscr{V}^h\) and \(\delta^h\) that are composed of such functions are called piecewise linear finite element spaces.
  • A general case in which \(\mathscr{V}^h\) is \(n\) dimensional, we partition the domain \([0,1]\) into \(n\) nonoverlapping subintervals. The typical subinterval is denoted by \([x_A,x_{A+1}]\), where \(x_A < x_{A+1}\) and \(A=1,2,\ldots,n\).
  • The \(x_A\)’s are called nodal points, or simply nodes. The subintervals are referred to as elements.
  • The mesh parameter, \(h\), is taken to be the maximum element length.

Shape Functions

  • Associate to a typical internal node (i.e., \(2\leq A \leq N\))

    \begin{align} N_A(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{(x - x_{A-1})}{h_{A-1}}, \hspace{2em} & x_{A-1} \leq x \leq x_A \\ \frac{(x_{A+1} - x)}{h_A}, & x_A \leq x \leq x_{A+1} \\ 0, & \mathrm{elsewhere} \end{cases} \end{align}

    whereas for the boundary nodes we have \(N_1(x) = \frac{x_2 - x}{h_1}\), and \(N_{n+1}(x) = \frac{x-x_n}{h_n}\).

  • The shape functions are also referred to as “hat”, or “roof” functions.

  • Note that \(N_A(x_B) = \delta_{AB}\), where \(\delta_{AB}\) is the Kroenecker delta (i.e, \(\delta_{AB} = 1\) if \(A = B\), whereas \(\delta_{AB} = 0\) if \(A\neq B\)).

  • A typical member \(w^h \in \mathscr{V}^h\) has the form \(\sum_{A=1}^n c_A N_A\) and appear as below.

  • Note that while \(w^h\) is continuous, it has discontinuous slope across each element boundary, i.e. its derivative \(w_{,x}^h\) will be piecewise constant.
  • Typical members of \(\delta^h\) are obtained by adding \(\mathscr{g}^h = \mathscr{g}N_{n+1}\) to a typical member of \(\mathscr{V}^h\).

(\(\S1.9\)) Properties of \(\boldsymbol{K}\)

  • The shape functions \(N_A,\, A = 1,2,\ldots,n+1\), are zero outside of the immediate neighborhood of node \(A\). As a results, many of the entries of \(\boldsymbol{K}\) are zero.
  • Let \(B > A+1\). Then \[ K_{AB} = \int_0^1 \underbrace{N_{A,x} N_{B,x}}_0 dx = 0 \]
  • It can readily be seen that \(\boldsymbol{K}\) is symmetric, i.e. \(K_{AB} = K_{BA}\).

  • With a matrix structure with zeros outside the diagonal band, \(\boldsymbol{K}\) is said to be banded.

Definition. An \(n\times n\) matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is said to be positive definite if

  1. \(\boldsymbol{c}^T \boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{c} \geq 0\) for all \(n\) vectors \(\boldsymbol{c}\); and
  2. \(\boldsymbol{c}^T \boldsymbol{A} \boldsymbol{c} = 0\) implies \(\boldsymbol{c} = \boldsymbol{0}\).


  • It can be shown that the stiffness matrix has the above property, i.e. the \(n\times n\) matrix \(\boldsymbol{K}\) is positive definite.
  • A positive-definite matrix posesses a unique inverse (it can be inverted).
  • The eigenvalues of a positive-definite matrix are real and positive.
  • Summary: The stiffness matrix defined above is
    1. Symmetric
    2. Banded
    3. Positive-definite

(\(\S1.12\)) The Element Point of View

  • Up till now the Galerkin approximation method implied the selection of a finite number of basis functions without a general restriction on their region of influence.
  • Even when we restricted the basis functions to have influence only up to the nearest neighboring node(s), our approach may be termed as a global point of view.
  • Now, we would like to come to the crux of the matter, by bringing the local or element point of view.

What is a Finite Element?

  • Two separate descriptions of the “finite element” will be provided based on the use of global versus local coordinates.
  • Linear (two-noded) finite element (global coordinates)

  • An individual element consists of the following quantities:

    (g1) Domain: \([x_A,\,x_{A+1}]\)
    (g2) Nodes: \(\{x_A,\,x_{A+1}\}\)
    (g3) Degrees of freedom: \(\{d_A,\,d_{A+1}\}\)
    (g4) Shape functions: \(\{N_A,\,N_{A+1}\}\)
    (g5) Interpolation function:  

    \[ u^h(x) = N_A(x) d_A + N_{A+1}(x) d_{A+1} \]

  • It is fruitful to introduce a local coordinate system \(tau\), and a set of local quantities, corresponding to the global ones, so that calculations for a typical element may be standardized.
  • Linear (two-noded) finite element (local coordinates)

  • An individual element consists of the following quantities:

    (l1) Domain: \([\xi_1,\,\xi_2]\)
    (l2) Nodes: \(\{\xi_1,\,\xi_2\}\)
    (l3) Degrees of freedom: \(\{d_1,\,d_2\}\)
    (l4) Shape functions: \(\{N_1,\,N_2\}\)
    (l5) Interpolation function:  

    \[ u^h(\xi) = N_1(\xi) d_1 + N_2(\xi) d_2 \]

What is an “Affine” Transformation

  • In relating the global and local coordinates, an “affine” transformation \(\xi:[x_A,x_{A+1}] \rightarrow [\xi_1,\xi_2]\), such that \(\xi(x_A) = \xi_1\) and \(\xi(x_{A+1}) = \xi_2\), where we take \(\xi_1 = -1\) and \(\xi_2 = +1\).

  • An affine (linear) mapping is provided by \[ \xi(x) = c_1 + c_2 x \] where \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are determined by

    \begin{align} \begin{array}{rl} -1 & = c_1 + x_A c_2 \\ 1 & = c_1 + x_{A+1} c_2 \end{array} \end{align}

  • Solving this system yields the mapping \(\xi(x)\)

    \begin{equation} \xi(x) = (2x - x_A - x_{A+1})\,/\,{h_A} \label{eq:mapforw} \end{equation}
  • The inverse mapping \(x(\xi)\) can be obtained by solving the above for \(x\) \[ x(\xi) = (h_A \xi + x_A + x_{A+1})\,/\,{2} \]
  • In \(\xi(x)\), \(\xi\) is a mapping and \(x\) is a point, whereas in \(x(\xi)\), \(x\) is a mapping and \(\xi\) is a point.

Notational Convention

  • lowercase subscripts pertain to the local numbering system, e.g. \(N_a\), or \(d_b\), etc.
  • UPPERCASE subscripts pertain to the global numbering system, e.g. \(F_A\), or \(N_B\), etc.
  • A superscript \(e\) denoted a quantitiy in the local description associated with element number \(e\), e.g. \[ d_a^e = d_A, \hspace{1em} N_a^e(\xi) = N_A(x^e(\xi)), \] where \[ x^e: [\xi_1, \xi_2] \rightarrow [x_1^e, x_2^e] = [x_A, x_{A+1}]. \]

Shape Functions (Local Description)

  • In terms of the local coordinate \(\xi\), the shape functions take a standard form \[ N_a(\xi) = \frac{1}{2} (1+ \xi_a \xi), \hspace{2em} a=1,\,2 \]

  • The local and global descriptions of the $e$th element are depicted below

Isoparametric Mapping

  • The mapping from the local to the global coordinates can be expressed in terms of the shape functions! \[ x^e(\xi) = \sum_{a=1}^2 N_a(\xi) x_a^e \]
  • The shape function derivatives can be taken as \[ N_{a,\xi} = \frac{\xi_a}{2} = \frac{(-1)^a}{2} \] which are used to write \[ x_{,\xi}^e = \frac{h^e}{2} \] where \(h^e = x_2^e - x_1^e\)
  • The derivative of the inverse mapping can easily be found as \[ \xi_{,x}^e = (x_{,\xi}^e)^{-1} = \frac{2}{h^e} \]

(\(\S1.13\)) Stiffness Matrix and Force Vector

  • The difference between global and local become clear by looking at the two alternative approaches to the construction of the stiffness matrix and the force vector.

  • Start with the global approach
    Assume that the model consists of \(n_{el}\) elements, numbered as shown below

  • Recall the definitions of the (global) stiffness matrix and force vector \[ \boldsymbol{K} = \underbrace{[K_{AB}]}_{n\times n}, \hspace{2em} \boldsymbol{F} = \underbrace{\{F_A\}}_{n\times 1} \]

Components of the global stiffness matrix, \(K_{AB}\), and force vector, \(F_A\) are calculated by taking integrals over the domain, \(\Omega\) as

  • \[ K_{AB} = a(N_A, N_B) = \int_0^1 N_{A,x} N_{B,x} dx \]
  • \begin{align} F_A & = (N_A, \mathscr{f}) + \delta_{1A} \mathscr{h} - a(N_A, N_{n+1}) \mathscr{g} \\ & = \int_0^1 N_A \mathscr{f} dx + \delta_{1A} \mathscr{h} - \int_0^1 N_{A,x} N_{n+1,x} dx\, \mathscr{g} \end{align}

  • Contributions from the left, mid and right node should be illustrated on board!

If the integrals over the domain \([0,1]\) are written as a sum piecewise sum of terms out of the adjacent subdomains, i.e. \([x_A,x_{A+1}]\), the so-called element domains, we see

\[ \begin{align} \boldsymbol{K} = \sum_{e=1}^{n_{el}} \boldsymbol{K}^e, \hspace{2em} & \boldsymbol{K}^e = [K_{AB}^e] \\ \boldsymbol{F} = \sum_{e=1}^{n_{el}} \boldsymbol{F}^e, \hspace{2em} & \boldsymbol{F}^e = [F_{A}^e] \end{align} \]

where

\[ \begin{align} {K}_{AB}^e & = a(N_A, N_B)^e = \int_{\Omega^e} N_{A,x} N_{B,x} dx \\ {F}_A^e & = (N_A,\mathscr{f})^e + \delta_{1e} \delta_{1A} \mathscr{h} - a(N_A,N_{n+1})^e \, \mathscr{g} \\ & = \int_{\Omega^e} N_A \mathscr{f} dx + \delta_{1e} \delta_{1A} \mathscr{h} - \int_{\Omega^e} N_{A,x} N_{n+1,x} dx \mathscr{g} \end{align} \]

and \(\Omega^e = [x_1^e,x_2^e]\), denote the eth element/subdomain. (Also note that the Kroenecker symbol \(\delta_{1e}\) is required to ensure that the only contribution is from the respective element.)

An important observation to make is that \(\boldsymbol{K}\) and \(\boldsymbol{F}\) can be constructed by summing the contributions of elemental matrices and vectors, respectively. In the literature, this procedure is sometimes called the direct stiffness method.

In practice, we do not carry the full matrix containing many zeros but merely add in the nonzero terms to the appropriate locations. For this purpose, it is useful to define the eth element stiffness matrix, \(\boldsymbol{k}^e\) and element force vector, \(\boldsymbol{f}^e\) as follows:

\[ \begin{align} & \boldsymbol{k}^e = \underbrace{[k_{ab}^e]}_{2\times 2}\,, \hspace{2em} \boldsymbol{f}^e = \underbrace{\{f_a^e\}}_{2\times 1}\,, \hspace{2em} a,b=1,2 \\ & k_{ab}^e = a(N_a, N_b)^e = \int_{\Omega^e} N_{a,x} N_{b,x} dx\\ & f_a^e = \int_{\Omega^e} N_a \mathscr{f} dx + \begin{cases} \delta_{1a} \mathscr{h} \hspace{2em} & e = 1\\ 0 & e = 2,3,\ldots,n_{el}-1 \\ -k_{a2}^e \mathscr{g} & e = n_{el} \end{cases} \end{align} \]

It is important to understand that while \(\boldsymbol{k}^e\) and \(\boldsymbol{f}^e\) are defined with respect to the local ordering, \(\boldsymbol{K}^e\) and \(\boldsymbol{F}^e\) are defined with respect to the global ordering. To determine the addressing or mapping of the components of \(\boldsymbol{k}^e\) and \(\boldsymbol{f}^e\) into the global forms, additional information will be injected into the data structures.

(\(\S1.15\)) Explicit Computation of Element Arrays

Two important rules from calculus will make it possible to transform the stiffness and force array computations from global to local coordinates.

Change of Integration Variable

Let \(\mathscr{f}:[x_1,x_2] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) be an integrable function and let \(x:[\xi_1, \xi_2] \to [x_1, x_2]\) be continuously differentiable, with \(x(\xi_1) = x_1\) and \(x(\xi_2) = x_2\). Then \[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x) dx = \int_{\xi_1}^{\xi_2} f(x(\xi)) x_{,\xi}(\xi) d\xi \]

Chain Rule

Let \(\mathscr{f}\) and \(x\) as above, and, in addition, assume \(\mathscr{f}\) is differentiable. Then \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \xi} \mathscr{f}(x(\xi)) = \mathscr{f}_{,x}(x(\xi)) x_{,\xi}(\xi) \]

Computation of \(\boldsymbol{k}^e\)

  • \begin{align} k_{ab}^e & = \int_{\Omega^e} N_{a,x}(x) N_{b,x}(x) dx \\ & = \int_{-1}^{+1} N_{a,x}(x(\xi)) N_{b,x}(x(\xi))\, x_{,\xi}(\xi) d\xi \\ & = \int_{-1}^{+1} N_{a,\xi}(\xi) N_{b,\xi}(\xi)\, x_{,\xi}(\xi)^{-1} d\xi \\ & = (-1)^{a+b}/h^e \end{align}
  • Thus \[ \boldsymbol{k}^e = \frac{1}{h^e} \left[ \begin{array}{rr} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{array} \right] \]
  • Observe that \(N_{a,\xi}\) is independent of any element particulars, thus the it does not have to be computed for each element.
  • But the derivatives \(x_{,\xi}\) and \(\xi_{,x}\) do depent on element particulars (in the present case \(h^e\)).

Computation of \(\boldsymbol{f}^e\)

  • Suprisingly this requires more intuition than the stiffness computation.
  • The integral involves a given \(\mathscr{f}\) which may change from case to case, inhibiting automation of element computations.

  • Generally, a convenient approximation is made, for example, by replacing \(\mathscr{f}\) by its linear interpolate over each element, namely, \[ \mathscr{f}^h = \sum_{a=1}^2 \mathscr{f}_a N_a \] where \(\mathscr{f}_a = \mathscr{f} (x(\xi_a))\); see figure below.

The notation \(\mathscr{f}^h\) is used to indicate that the approximation depends on the mesh. Based on this, standardization of input may be facilitated as

  • \begin{align} \int_{\Omega_e} N_a(x) \mathscr{f}^h(x) dx & = \int_{-1}^{+1} N_a(x(\xi)) \mathscr{f}^h(x(\xi))\, x_{,\xi}(\xi) d\xi \\ & = \frac{h_e}{2} \int_{-1}^{+1} N_a(\xi) \sum_{b=1}^2 \mathscr{f}_b N_b(\xi)\, d\xi \\ & = \frac{h_e}{2} \sum_{b=1}^2 \int_{-1}^{+1} N_a(\xi) N_b(\xi) d\xi \, \mathscr{f}_b \end{align}
  • Carrying out the integrations (\(\int_{-1}^{+1} N_a N_b d\xi = (1 + \delta_{ab})/3\)) yields \[ \mathscr{f}^e = \frac{h^e}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix} \mathscr{f}_1 \\ \mathscr{f}_2 \end{Bmatrix} = \frac{h^e}{6} \begin{Bmatrix} 2 \mathscr{f}_1 + \mathscr{f}_2 \\ \mathscr{f}_1 + 2 \mathscr{f}_2 \end{Bmatrix} \hspace{1em} (+ \,\mathrm{boundary}\,\mathrm{terms}) \]
